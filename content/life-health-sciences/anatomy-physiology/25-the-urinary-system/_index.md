**Chapter Objectives**

After studying this chapter, you will be able to:

- Describe the composition of urine
- Label structures of the urinary system
- Characterize the roles of each of the parts of the urinary system
- Illustrate the macroscopic and microscopic structures of the kidney
- Trace the flow of blood through the kidney
- Outline how blood is filtered in the kidney nephron
- Provide symptoms of kidney failure
- List some of the solutes filtered, secreted, and reabsorbed in different parts of the nephron
- Describe the role of a portal system in the kidney
- Explain how urine osmolarity is hormonally regulated
- Describe the regulation of major ions by the kidney
- Summarize the role of the kidneys in maintaining acid–base balance

The urinary system has roles you may be well aware of: cleansing the blood and
ridding the body of wastes probably come to mind. However, there are additional,
equally important functions played by the system. Take for example, regulation
of pH, a function shared with the lungs and the buffers in the blood.
Additionally, the regulation of blood pressure is a role shared with the heart
and blood vessels. What about regulating the concentration of solutes in the
blood? Did you know that the kidney is important in determining the
concentration of red blood cells? Eighty-five percent of the erythropoietin
(EPO) produced to stimulate red blood cell production is produced in the
kidneys. The kidneys also perform the final synthesis step of vitamin D
production, converting calcidiol to calcitriol, the active form of vitamin D.

If the kidneys fail, these functions are compromised or lost altogether, with
devastating effects on homeostasis. The affected individual might experience
weakness, lethargy, shortness of breath, anemia, widespread edema (swelling),
metabolic acidosis, rising potassium levels, heart arrhythmias, and more. Each
of these functions is vital to your well-being and survival. The urinary system,
controlled by the nervous system, also stores urine until a convenient time for
disposal and then provides the anatomical structures to transport this waste
liquid to the outside of the body. Failure of nervous control or the anatomical
structures leading to a loss of control of urination results in a condition
called incontinence.

This chapter will help you to understand the anatomy of the urinary system and
how it enables the physiologic functions critical to homeostasis. It is best to
think of the kidney as a regulator of plasma makeup rather than simply a urine
producer. As you read each section, ask yourself this question: “What happens if
this does not work?” This question will help you to understand how the urinary
system maintains homeostasis and affects all the other systems of the body and
the quality of one’s life.

## Sections

- **Physical Characteristics of Urine** — how blood plasma, glomerular filtrate, and urine compare and contrast, and the characteristics of a normal urine sample, including its normal range of pH, osmolarity, and volume.
- **Gross Anatomy of Urine Transport** — the location, structure, histology, and function of the ureters, urinary bladder, and urethra, how the male and female urethras compare, the micturition reflex, and the voluntary and involuntary neural control of micturition.
- **Gross Anatomy of the Kidney** — the external structure of the kidney, its major internal divisions and structures, its blood vessels and the path of blood through it, how cortical and juxtamedullary nephrons compare, and the structures and physiological characteristics of the cortex and medulla.
- **Microscopic Anatomy of the Kidney** — the histological differences between the renal cortex and medulla, the filtration membrane, the renal corpuscles, renal tubules, and renal capillaries, the peritubular capillaries and vasa recta, the juxtaglomerular apparatus, and the histology of the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting ducts.
- **Physiology of Urine Formation** — the hydrostatic and colloid osmotic forces that favor and oppose filtration, the glomerular filtration rate and the factors that raise or lower it, the percent of filtrate normally reabsorbed and why reabsorption matters, how to calculate daily urine production, and the common symptoms of kidney failure.
- **Tubular Reabsorption** — the transport mechanisms and membrane proteins of the nephron, passive and active tubular reabsorption, why the differential permeability of nephron segments is necessary for urine formation, how and where water, organic compounds, and ions are reabsorbed, the countercurrent multiplication mechanisms that concentrate urine, and where tubular secretion occurs.
- **Regulation of Renal Blood Flow** — the myogenic and tubuloglomerular feedback mechanisms and how they affect urine volume and composition, and the function of the juxtaglomerular apparatus.
- **Endocrine Regulation of Kidney Function** — the extrinsic control of GFR by the renin–angiotensin mechanism, natriuretic peptides, and sympathetic adrenergic activity, how the renin–angiotensin system, aldosterone, antidiuretic hormone, and natriuretic peptides regulate reabsorption and secretion, and the roles of other hormones in kidney control.
- **Regulation of Fluid Volume and Composition** — the mechanism of action of diuretics, and why the differential permeability of specific sections of the nephron tubules is necessary for urine formation.
- **The Urinary System and Homeostasis** — the role of the kidneys in vitamin D activation and in regulating erythropoiesis, examples of how the urinary system responds to maintain homeostasis and relates to other body systems, and the factors and problems that could disrupt homeostasis when the urinary system cannot maintain it.
