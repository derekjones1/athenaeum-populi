**Chapter Objectives**

After studying this chapter, you will be able to:

- Identify the primary functions of blood, its fluid and cellular components, and its physical characteristics
- Identify the most important proteins and other solutes present in blood plasma
- Describe the formation of the formed element components of blood
- Discuss the structure and function of red blood cells and hemoglobin
- Classify and characterize white blood cells
- Describe the structure of platelets and explain the process of hemostasis
- Explain the significance of ABO and Rh blood groups in blood transfusions
- Discuss a variety of blood disorders
{{< /callout >}}

Single-celled organisms do not need blood. They obtain nutrients directly from
and excrete wastes directly into their environment. The human organism cannot
do that. Our large, complex bodies need blood to deliver nutrients to and
remove wastes from our trillions of cells. The heart pumps blood throughout the
body in a network of blood vessels. Together, these three components—blood,
heart, and vessels—make up the cardiovascular system. This chapter focuses on
the medium of transport: blood.

## Sections

- **An Overview of Blood** — the primary functions of blood in transportation, defense, and homeostasis, its fluid component and three types of formed elements and their proportions in a sample, its unique physical characteristics, and the composition of plasma with its key solutes and plasma proteins.
- **Production of the Formed Elements** — how the formed elements arise from bone marrow stem cells, and the role of hemopoietic growth factors in promoting their production.
- **Erythrocytes** — the anatomy of erythrocytes, the steps in their life cycle, and the composition and function of hemoglobin.
- **Leukocytes and Platelets** — the general characteristics of leukocytes, their classification by lineage, structure, and function, the most common leukocyte malignancies, and the lineage, structure, and function of platelets.
- **Hemostasis** — the three mechanisms of hemostasis, how the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways lead to the common pathway with the coagulation factors of each, and the disorders affecting hemostasis.
- **Blood Typing** — the two basic physiological consequences of transfusing incompatible blood, how the ABO and Rh groups compare, which blood groups can be safely transfused into patients of different ABO types, and the pathophysiology of hemolytic disease of the newborn.
