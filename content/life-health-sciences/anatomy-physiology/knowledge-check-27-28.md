{{< selfcheck question="Why does it matter for sperm production that the testes sit in the scrotum, and how do the dartos and cremaster muscles change the position of the testes and the surface area of the scrotum in cold weather, and what happens as the environmental temperature increases?" >}}
The testes are located in a skin-covered, highly pigmented, muscular sac called
the scrotum that extends from the body behind the penis. This location is
important in sperm production, which occurs within the testes, and proceeds more
efficiently when the testes are kept 2 to 4 °C below core body temperature. By
contracting simultaneously, the dartos and cremaster muscles can elevate the
testes in cold weather (or water), moving the testes closer to the body and
decreasing the surface area of the scrotum to retain heat. Alternatively, as the
environmental temperature increases, the scrotum relaxes, moving the testes
farther from the body core and increasing scrotal surface area, which promotes
heat loss.

{{< selfcheck question="How do birth control pills that provide a constant level of both estrogen and progesterone stop ovulation, and why can skipping even one or two pills lead to ovulation?" >}}
Birth control pills typically work by providing a constant level of both
estrogen and progesterone, which negatively feeds back onto the hypothalamus and
pituitary, thus preventing the release of FSH and LH. Without FSH, the follicles
do not mature, and without the LH surge, ovulation does not occur. Because birth
control pills function by providing constant estrogen and progesterone levels
and disrupting negative feedback, skipping even just one or two pills at certain
points of the cycle (or even being several hours late taking the pill) can lead
to an increase in FSH and LH and result in ovulation.

{{< selfcheck question="What makes a tissue of the embryo bipotential, and how does testosterone act on bipotential tissues, as in the example of the cells that form the glans?" >}}
In both male and female embryos, the same group of cells has the potential to
develop into either the male or female gonads; this tissue is considered
bipotential. Testosterone can influence tissues that are bipotential to become
male reproductive structures. For example, with exposure to testosterone, cells
that could become either the glans penis or the glans clitoris form the glans
penis. Without testosterone, these same cells differentiate into the clitoris.

{{< selfcheck question="How do dizygotic and monozygotic twins arise, and how does the stage at which a zygote splits decide whether monozygotic twins have individual placentas or share one?" >}}
In approximately 1 percent of ovulation cycles, two eggs are released and both
are fertilized. Because dizygotic twins develop from two eggs fertilized by two
sperm, they are no more identical than siblings born at different times. Much
less commonly, a zygote can divide into two separate offspring during early
development. This results in the birth of monozygotic (or identical) twins. The
twin embryos that separated at the two-cell stage will have individual
placentas, whereas twin embryos that form from separation at the blastocyst
stage will share a placenta and a chorionic cavity.

{{< selfcheck question="What does amniotic fluid consist of early in development, what does floating within it protect the embryo from, and what can the embryo do within the fluid?" >}}
Early in development, amniotic fluid consists almost entirely of a filtrate of
maternal plasma. Floating within the amniotic fluid, the embryo—and later, the
fetus—is protected from trauma and rapid temperature changes. It can move
freely within the fluid and can prepare for swallowing and breathing out of the
uterus.

{{< selfcheck question="What does the passage of meconium in the uterus signal, what reflex makes it more likely that the meconium will be inhaled into the fetal lungs, and why does meconium make bacterial infection more likely even though it is sterile?" >}}
The passage of meconium in the uterus signals fetal distress, particularly fetal
hypoxia (i.e., oxygen deprivation). Notably, fetal hypoxic stress also induces a
gasping reflex, increasing the likelihood that meconium will be inhaled into the
fetal lungs. Although meconium is a sterile substance, it interferes with the
antibiotic properties of the amniotic fluid and makes the newborn and pregnant
person more vulnerable to bacterial infections at birth and during the
perinatal period.

{{< selfcheck question="In the final stage of childbirth, what detaches the placenta from the uterine wall, what then reduces blood loss from the site of the placenta, and why must the expelled placenta and fetal membranes be examined to ensure that they are intact?" >}}
After expulsion of the newborn, the myometrium continues to contract. This
movement shears the placenta from the back of the uterine wall. It is then
easily delivered through the vagina. Continued uterine contractions then reduce
blood loss from the site of the placenta. It is important that the obstetrician
examines the expelled placenta and fetal membranes to ensure that they are
intact. If fragments of the placenta remain in the uterus, they can cause
postpartum hemorrhage.

{{< selfcheck question="How does breast milk help a newborn expel meconium and clear bilirubin, and why is a high level of circulating bilirubin dangerous to a newborn?" >}}
Breast milk has laxative properties that help expel meconium from the
intestines and clear bilirubin through the excretion of bile. A high
concentration of bilirubin in the blood causes jaundice. Some degree of
jaundice is normal in newborns, but a high level of bilirubin—which is
neurotoxic—can cause brain damage. Newborns, who do not yet have a fully
functional blood–brain barrier, are highly vulnerable to the bilirubin
circulating in the blood.

{{< selfcheck question="Compared with recessive lethal inheritance patterns, why are dominant lethal inheritance patterns much more rare, and how can a dominant lethal allele such as the allele for Huntington’s disease nevertheless be maintained in the human population?" >}}
Dominant lethal inheritance patterns are much more rare because neither
heterozygotes nor homozygotes survive. Of course, dominant lethal alleles that
arise naturally through mutation and cause miscarriages or stillbirths are never
transmitted to subsequent generations. However, some dominant lethal alleles,
such as the allele for Huntington’s disease, cause a shortened life span but may
not be identified until after the person reaches reproductive age and has
children. In this way, dominant lethal alleles can be maintained in the human
population.
