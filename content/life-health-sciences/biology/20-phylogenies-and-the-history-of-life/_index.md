are related. Both are members of the domain Eukarya and have cells
containing many similar organelles, genes, and proteins. (credit:

This bee and *Echinacea* flower, shown above, could not look more
different, yet they are related, as are all living organisms on Earth.
By following pathways of similarities and changes—both visible and
genetic—scientists seek to map the evolutionary past of how life
developed from single-celled organisms to the tremendous collection of
creatures that have germinated, crawled, floated, swum, flown, and
walked on this planet.

## Sections

- **Organizing Life on Earth** — why a comprehensive classification
  system is needed, the levels of the taxonomic hierarchy, how
  systematics and taxonomy relate to phylogeny, and what a phylogenetic
  tree's components represent.
- **Determining Evolutionary Relationships** — how scientists compare
  homologous and analogous traits, and how cladistics and the principle
  of maximum parsimony use those traits to build phylogenetic trees.
- **Perspectives on the Phylogenetic Tree** — how horizontal gene
  transfer moves genes across species, and how the web and ring models
  of phylogenetic relationships have challenged the original
  single-trunk tree concept.
