Lab-on-a-chip technology allows immunological assays to be
miniaturized so tests can be done rapidly with minimum quantities of
expensive reagents. The chips contain tiny flow tubes to allow
movement of fluids by capillary action, reaction sites with embedded
reagents, and data output through electronic sensors. (credit:
modification of work by Maggie Bartlett, NHGRI)
{{< /mediafigure >}}

Many laboratory tests are designed to confirm a presumptive diagnosis
by detecting antibodies specific to a suspected pathogen.
Unfortunately, many such tests are time-consuming and expensive. That
is now changing, however, with the development of new, miniaturized
technologies that are fast and inexpensive. For example, researchers
at Columbia University are developing a “lab-on-a-chip” technology
that will test a single drop of blood for 15 different infectious
diseases, including HIV and syphilis, in a matter of minutes (Chin,
Curtis D. et al., “Mobile Device for Disease Diagnosis and Data
Tracking in Resource-Limited Settings,” *Clinical Chemistry* 59, no. 4
(2013): 629-40). The blood is pulled through tiny capillaries into
reaction chambers where the patient’s antibodies mix with reagents. A
chip reader that attaches to a cell phone analyzes the results and
sends them to the patient’s healthcare provider. Currently the device
is being field tested in Rwanda to check pregnant people for chronic
diseases. Researchers estimate that the chip readers will sell for
about $100 and individual chips for $1 (Evarts, H., “Fast, Low-Cost
Device Uses the Cloud to Speed Up Testing for HIV and More,” January
24, 2013. Accessed July 14, 2016.).

## Sections

- **Polyclonal and Monoclonal Antibody Production** — how polyclonal
  antibodies are produced by immunizing an animal, how monoclonal
  antibodies are produced from a single hybridoma cell line, and why
  antibody cross-reactivity is less of a problem for monoclonal
  antibodies than for polyclonal ones.
- **Detecting Antigen-Antibody Complexes** — the assays used to detect
  antigen-antibody complexes in vitro, the circumstances under which
  antigen-antibody complexes precipitate out of solution, and how
  antibodies in a patient's serum can be used to diagnose disease.
- **Agglutination Assays** — the difference between direct and indirect
  agglutination, the uses of hemagglutination in diagnosing disease,
  and how blood types are determined and cross-matched before a
  transfusion.
- **EIAs and ELISAs** — the differences and similarities among EIA,
  FEIA, and ELISA, the difference between immunohistochemistry and
  immunocytochemistry, and the different purposes of direct and
  indirect ELISA.
- **Fluorescent Antibody Techniques** — the benefits of
  immunofluorescent antibody assays over nonfluorescent assays, the
  difference between direct and indirect fluorescent antibody assays,
  and how a flow cytometer and a fluorescence-activated cell sorter
  are used to quantify and separate specific subsets of cells.
