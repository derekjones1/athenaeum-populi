Leprosy has been a devastating disease throughout much of human history. Aside
from the symptoms and complications of the illness, its social stigma led
sufferers to be cast out of communities and isolated in colonies; in some
regions this practice lasted well into the twentieth century. At that time, the
best potential treatment for leprosy was oil from the chaulmoogra tree, but the
oil was extremely thick, causing blisters and making usage painful and
ineffective. Healthcare professionals seeking a better application contacted
Alice Ball, a young chemist at the University of Hawaii, who had focused her
master’s thesis on a similar plant. Ball initiated a sequence of procedures
(repeated acidification and purification to change the characteristics of the
oil and isolate the active substances (esters, discussed later in this text)).
The “Ball Method” as it later came to be called, became the standard treatment
for leprosy for decades. In the liquid and solid states, atomic and molecular
interactions are of considerable strength and play an important role in
determining a number of physical properties of the substance. For example, the
thickness, or viscosity, of the chaulmoogra oil was due to its intermolecular
forces. In this chapter, the nature of these interactions and their effects on
various physical properties of liquid and solid phases will be examined.

## Sections

- **Intermolecular Forces** — describing the types of intermolecular forces possible between atoms or molecules in condensed phases (dispersion forces, dipole-dipole attractions, and hydrogen bonding); identifying the types of intermolecular forces experienced by specific molecules based on their structures; and explaining the relation between the intermolecular forces present within a substance and the temperatures associated with changes in its physical state.
- **Properties of Liquids** — distinguishing between adhesive and cohesive forces; defining viscosity, surface tension, and capillary rise; and describing the roles of intermolecular attractive forces in each of these properties and phenomena.
- **Phase Transitions** — defining phase transitions and phase transition temperatures; explaining the relation between phase transition temperatures and intermolecular attractive forces; and describing the processes represented by typical heating and cooling curves, and computing heat flows and enthalpy changes accompanying these processes.
- **Phase Diagrams** — explaining the construction and use of a typical phase diagram; using phase diagrams to identify stable phases at given temperatures and pressures, and to describe phase transitions resulting from changes in these properties; and describing the supercritical fluid phase of matter.
- **The Solid State of Matter** — defining and describing the bonding and properties of ionic, molecular, metallic, and covalent network crystalline solids; describing the main types of crystalline solids: ionic solids, metallic solids, covalent network solids, and molecular solids; and explaining the ways in which crystal defects can occur in a solid.
- **Lattice Structures in Crystalline Solids** — describing the arrangement of atoms and ions in crystalline structures; computing ionic radii using unit cell dimensions; and explaining the use of X-ray diffraction measurements in determining crystalline structures.
