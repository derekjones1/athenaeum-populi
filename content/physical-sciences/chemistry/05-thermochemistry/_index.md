Chemical reactions, such as those that occur when you light a match, involve
changes in energy as well as matter. Societies at all levels of development
could not function without the energy released by chemical reactions. In 2012,
about 85% of US energy consumption came from the combustion of petroleum
products, natural gas, coal, wood, and garbage. We use this energy to produce electricity
(38%); to transport food, raw materials, manufactured goods, and people (27%);
for industrial production (21%); and to heat and power our homes and
businesses (10%). (US Energy Information Administration, *Primary Energy
Consumption by Source and Sector, 2012*. Data derived from US Energy
Information Administration, *Monthly Energy Review* (January 2014).) While
these combustion reactions help us meet our essential energy needs, they are
also recognized by the majority of the scientific community as a major
contributor to global climate change.

Useful forms of energy are also available from a variety of chemical
reactions other than combustion. For example, the energy produced by the
batteries in a cell phone, car, or flashlight results from chemical
reactions. This chapter introduces many of the basic ideas necessary to
explore the relationships between chemical changes and energy, with a focus
on thermal energy.

*Source note:* the source's list of fuels leaves out natural gas, which the 85% includes: in the US Energy Information Administration's figures for 2012, petroleum, coal, and biomass together supplied about 61% of primary energy consumption and natural gas about 28% ([Monthly Energy Review, Table 1.3](https://www.eia.gov/totalenergy/data/monthly/)).

## Sections

- **Energy Basics** — defining energy, distinguishing types of energy, and the energy changes that accompany chemical and physical changes; heat, thermal energy, and temperature; specific heat and heat capacity; and calculations involving heat, specific heat, and temperature change.
- **Calorimetry** — the technique of calorimetry, and calculating and interpreting heat and related properties from typical calorimetry data.
- **Enthalpy** — the first law of thermodynamics, enthalpy as a state function, writing and balancing thermochemical equations, calculating enthalpy changes for chemical reactions, and Hess’s law.
