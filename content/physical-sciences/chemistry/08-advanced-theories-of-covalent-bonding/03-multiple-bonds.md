The hybrid orbital model appears to account well for the geometry of molecules involving single covalent bonds. Is it also capable of describing molecules containing double and triple bonds? We have already discussed that multiple bonds consist of sigma (σ) and pi (π) bonds. Next we can consider how we visualize these components and how they relate to hybrid orbitals. The Lewis structure of ethene, C₂H₄, shows us that each carbon atom is surrounded by one other carbon atom and two hydrogen atoms.

{{< mediafigure src="chemistry/CNX_Chem_08_03_C4H4Lewis_img" kind="diagram" eager="true" alt="A Lewis structure of ethene: two carbon atoms double-bonded to each other, each carbon atom also single-bonded to two hydrogen atoms, one above and one below it. No atom has a lone pair." >}}
{{< /mediafigure >}}

The three bonding regions form a trigonal planar electron-pair geometry. Thus we expect the sigma (σ) bonds from each carbon atom are formed using a set of sp² hybrid orbitals that result from hybridization of two of the 2p orbitals and the 2s orbital (shown in the energy diagram below). These orbitals form the C–H single bonds and the sigma (σ) bond in the C=C double bond (shown in the drawing of the bonds of ethene below). The pi (π) bond in the C=C double bond results from the overlap of the third (remaining) 2p orbital on each carbon atom that is not involved in hybridization. This unhybridized p orbital (lobes shown in red and blue in the drawing of the bonds of ethene below) is perpendicular to the plane of the sp² hybrid orbitals. Thus the unhybridized 2p orbitals overlap in a side-by-side fashion, above and below the internuclear axis (shown in the same drawing) and form a pi (π) bond.

{{< mediafigure src="chemistry/CNX_Chem_08_03_sp3config" kind="diagram" alt="An orbital energy diagram, energy E rising up an arrow at the left. Left, headed Orbitals in an isolated C atom: at the bottom a line labeled s holding an up arrow and a down arrow; far above it three lines labeled p, the first two holding one up arrow each and the third empty, with the word Unhybridized beside them. A gray arrow labeled Hybridization points right. Right, headed Orbitals in the sp² hybridized C atom in C₂H₄: three lines labeled sp², each holding one up arrow, and above them one line labeled p holding one up arrow." >}}
In ethene, each carbon atom is sp² hybridized, and the sp² orbitals and the p orbital are singly occupied. The hybrid orbitals overlap to form sigma (σ) bonds, while the p orbitals on each carbon atom overlap to form a pi (π) bond.
{{< /mediafigure >}}

(a) Two carbon atoms, shown as dots, each with three yellow lobes in the plane. Each carbon atom also has a red lobe above the plane and a blue lobe below it, dashed lines joining the two red lobes and the two blue lobes. (b) Without the hydrogen atoms, the red lobes merge into one region above the plane and the blue lobes into one below it, both labeled One π bond." >}}
In the ethene molecule, C₂H₄, there are (a) five sigma (σ) bonds. One C–C sigma (σ) bond results from overlap of sp² hybrid orbitals on the carbon atom with one sp² hybrid orbital on the other carbon atom. Four C–H bonds result from the overlap between the C atoms’ sp² orbitals with s orbitals on the hydrogen atoms. (b) The pi (π) bond is formed by the side-by-side overlap of the two unhybridized p orbitals in the two carbon atoms. The two lobes of the pi (π) bond are above and below the plane of the sigma (σ) system.
{{< /mediafigure >}}

In an ethene molecule, the four hydrogen atoms and the two carbon atoms are all in the same plane. If the two planes of sp² hybrid orbitals tilted relative to each other, the p orbitals would not be oriented to overlap efficiently to create the pi (π) bond. The planar configuration for the ethene molecule occurs because it is the most stable bonding arrangement. This is a significant difference between sigma (σ) and pi (π) bonds; rotation around single sigma (σ) bonds occurs easily because the end-to-end orbital overlap does not depend on the relative orientation of the orbitals on each atom in the bond. In other words, rotation around the internuclear axis does not change the extent to which the sigma (σ) bonding orbitals overlap because the bonding electron density is symmetric about the axis. Rotation about the internuclear axis is much more difficult for multiple bonds since this would drastically decrease the off-axis overlap of the pi (π) bonding orbitals, essentially breaking the pi (π) bond.

In molecules with sp hybrid orbitals, two unhybridized p orbitals remain on the atom (shown in the drawing of a carbon atom’s sp orbitals below). We find this situation in acetylene, H–C≡C–H, which is a linear molecule. The sp hybrid orbitals of the two carbon atoms overlap end to end to form a sigma (σ) bond between the carbon atoms (shown in the drawing of the bonds of acetylene below). The remaining sp orbitals form sigma (σ) bonds with hydrogen atoms. The two unhybridized p orbitals per carbon are positioned such that they overlap side by side and, hence, form two pi (π) bonds. The two carbon atoms of acetylene are thus bound together by one sigma (σ) bond and two pi (π) bonds, giving a triple bond.

Hybridization involves only sigma (σ) bonds, lone pairs of electrons, and single unpaired electrons (radicals). Structures that account for these features describe the correct hybridization of the atoms. However, many structures also include resonance forms. Remember that resonance forms occur when various arrangements of pi (π) bonds are possible. Since the arrangement of pi (π) bonds involves only the unhybridized orbitals, resonance does not influence the assignment of hybridization.

For example, molecule benzene has two resonance forms (shown in the figure below). We can use either of these forms to determine that each of the carbon atoms is bonded to three other atoms with no lone pairs, so the correct hybridization is sp². The electrons in the unhybridized p orbitals form pi (π) bonds. Neither resonance structure completely describes the electrons in the pi (π) bonds. They are not located in one position or the other, but in reality are delocalized throughout the ring. Valence bond theory does not easily address delocalization. Bonding in molecules with resonance forms is better described by molecular orbital theory. (See the next section, [Section 8.4 Molecular Orbital Theory](/physical-sciences/chemistry/08-advanced-theories-of-covalent-bonding/04-molecular-orbital-theory/).)

Each is a ring of six carbon atoms, each carbon atom single-bonded to one hydrogen atom outside the ring, with three double and three single carbon–carbon bonds alternating around the ring. Every ring bond that is double in the left-hand structure is single in the right-hand one, and every single bond double. No atom has a lone pair." >}}
Each carbon atom in benzene, C₆H₆, is sp² hybridized, independently of which resonance form is considered. The electrons in the pi (π) bonds are not located in one set of p orbitals or the other, but rather delocalized throughout the molecule.
{{< /mediafigure >}}

Sulfur dioxide, SO₂, is a major component of volcanic gases as well as a product of the combustion of sulfur-containing coal. What is the hybridization of the S atom in SO₂?

## Summary

- Multiple bonds consist of a sigma (σ) bond located along the axis between two atoms and one or two pi (π) bonds. The sigma (σ) bonds are usually formed by the overlap of hybridized atomic orbitals, while the pi (π) bonds are formed by the side-by-side overlap of unhybridized orbitals. Resonance occurs when there are multiple unhybridized orbitals with the appropriate alignment to overlap, so the placement of pi (π) bonds can vary.
