Oxygen molecules orient randomly most of the time, as shown in the top magnified view. However, when we pour liquid oxygen through a magnet, the molecules line up with the magnetic field, and the attraction allows them to stay suspended between the poles of the magnet where the magnetic field is strongest. Other diatomic molecules (like N₂) flow past the magnet. The detailed explanation of bonding described in this chapter allows us to understand this phenomenon. We have examined the basic ideas of bonding, showing that atoms share electrons
to form molecules with stable Lewis structures and that we can predict the
shapes of those molecules by valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR)
theory. These ideas provide an important starting point for understanding
chemical bonding. But these models sometimes fall short in their abilities to
predict the behavior of real substances. How can we reconcile the geometries of
s, p, and d atomic orbitals with molecular shapes that show angles like
120° and 109.5°? Furthermore, we know that electrons and magnetic behavior are
related through electromagnetic fields. Both N₂ and O₂ have fairly similar Lewis
structures that contain lone pairs of electrons.

{{< mediafigure src="chemistry/CNX_Chem_08_00_N2O2Lewis_img" kind="diagram" alt="Two Lewis structures side by side. Left: two nitrogen atoms triple-bonded, each with one lone pair on its outer side. Right: two oxygen atoms double-bonded, each with two lone pairs, one above it and one on its outer side." >}}

Yet oxygen demonstrates very different magnetic behavior than nitrogen. We can
pour liquid nitrogen through a magnetic field with no visible interactions,
while liquid oxygen (shown in the photograph at the top of this page) is attracted to the magnet and
floats in the magnetic field. We need to understand the additional concepts of
valence bond theory, orbital hybridization, and molecular orbital theory to
understand these observations.

## Sections

- **Valence Bond Theory** — describing the formation of covalent bonds in terms of atomic orbital overlap, and defining and giving examples of σ and π bonds.
- **Hybrid Atomic Orbitals** — explaining the concept of atomic orbital hybridization and determining the hybrid orbitals associated with various molecular geometries.
- **Multiple Bonds** — describing multiple covalent bonding in terms of atomic orbital overlap, and relating the concept of resonance to π-bonding and electron delocalization.
- **Molecular Orbital Theory** — outlining the quantum-mechanical approach to deriving molecular orbitals from atomic orbitals; describing bonding and antibonding molecular orbitals; calculating bond orders from molecular electron configurations; writing molecular electron configurations for first- and second-row diatomic molecules; and relating these configurations to the molecules’ stabilities and magnetic properties.
